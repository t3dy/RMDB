Albertus Magnus (ca. 1193–1280): Medieval Natural Philosophy and the Legitimation of Magic

Life and Intellectual Context

Albertus Magnus (Albert of Cologne, ca. 1193–1280) was one of the most influential medieval philosophers and theologians, a Dominican friar whose vast erudition shaped medieval and Renaissance intellectual life. Born in Swabia, he joined the Dominican order and became a major theological authority, serving as Archbishop of Regensburg and influencing Thomas Aquinas, his most famous student.

Albertus’s significance for the history of magic lies not primarily in his personal engagement with magical practice, but in his reception: later Renaissance magicians and occultists claimed him as an authority on natural magic, astrology, and the hidden operations of nature. Whether Albertus intended his natural philosophy as magical justification remains debated; what is certain is that his writings on minerals, animals, plants, and celestial influences were cited by Renaissance magicians as foundational texts legitimating occult philosophy.

Actor Terminology: Albertus and Natural Philosophy

Albertus Magnus presented himself primarily as a natural philosopher (philosophus naturalis) engaged in comprehensive investigation of the natural world. His major works—the Summa Theologiae, Commentaries on the Sentences, and his monumental Summae on natural philosophy—were structured within scholastic theological frameworks.

Yet within this framework, Albertus engaged seriously with questions that would later be classified as magical:

  • The hidden properties of substances (proprietates occultae): How minerals, plants, and animals exert influences beyond obvious qualities
  • Astrological influence: How celestial bodies govern sublunary change through natural influence
  • The operations of intelligences: How celestial intelligences direct natural processes
  • Sympathetic correspondences: How substances at a distance can act on one another through hidden sympathies

Albertus emphasized experience (experientia) and observation as routes to understanding nature. He famously claimed to have dissected animals and examined minerals personally, grounding his natural philosophy in empirical investigation rather than pure textual authority. This empiricism, combined with his engagement with hidden properties and celestial influence, positioned him as an authority for later natural magicians.

The Question of Albertus’s Magical Texts

A substantial literature attributed to Albertus in the late medieval and Renaissance periods treated magic explicitly. Works with titles like Liber de Secretis (Book of Secrets), Liber Mineralium (Book of Minerals), and various compendia on talismans, magical practices, and occult correspondences circulated under Albertus’s name.

Textual scholarship has established that most of these texts were not written by Albertus but rather compiled, misattributed, or forged by later authors seeking to claim his authority. Yet the fact that these texts circulated as “Albertus’s” works reveals his reputation: he was understood by medieval and Renaissance readers as an authority on magic and hidden properties.

What Albertus actually wrote about natural philosophy could be appropriated for magical purposes. His discussions of hidden properties, astral influence, and sympathetic correspondences provided conceptual frameworks that magicians deployed. Later occultists did not need Albertus to have written explicit magical texts; they needed him to have provided philosophical legitimation for the investigation of nature’s hidden operations.

Albertus’s Natural Philosophy and Magic

Albertus’s genuine contributions to the natural philosophy relevant to magic include:

On celestial influence: Albertus engaged seriously with astrological theory, arguing that celestial bodies exert natural influence on sublunary things through their light and motion. The planets affect individual temperaments, bodily qualities, and life events. This was not demonic astrology but natural influence—entirely consonant with Christian theology.

On sympathetic action: Albertus discussed cases where substances at a distance exert mutual influence: the magnet attracting iron, the waxing moon affecting bodily humors, certain stones affecting the eye. These phenomena required explanation beyond mechanical causation; Albertus attributed them to hidden properties and sympathetic correspondences.

On the soul and form: Drawing on Aristotelian hylomorphism, Albertus distinguished between the material substance of a thing and its informing form or soul. Hidden properties derived from this formal principle; understanding the inner form permitted understanding why substances acted as they did.

On the hierarchy of nature: Albertus presented nature as hierarchically organized from God through angelic intelligences to celestial bodies to sublunary elemental things. Each level governs lower levels through natural influence. Understanding nature required understanding these correspondences and influences.

Reception in the Renaissance: Albertus as Authority

By the Renaissance, Albertus Magnus had become a towering authority figure in natural magic. When Agrippa, Paracelsus, and other Renaissance magicians sought to legitimize their investigations of hidden properties and magical correspondences, Albertus provided philosophical grounding.

Key aspects of Albertus’s authority:

  1. Scholastic legitimacy: Albertus was a recognized theological authority, a Dominican friar of undisputed orthodoxy. Attributing magical investigations to him created an argument that occult philosophy was compatible with Christian theology and ecclesiastical authority.

  2. Empiricism: Albertus’s emphasis on personal observation and experience provided a model for Renaissance investigators who claimed to base magical philosophy on direct investigation of nature rather than mere authority.

  3. Natural causation: Albertus’s framework of natural influence and hidden properties allowed magicians to describe their operations as natural rather than demonic. If celestial influence and sympathetic correspondences were real natural operations, then magical practice using these correspondences was natural magic, not forbidden goeteia.

  4. Comprehensive knowledge: Albertus’s monumental erudition and his ambition to encompass all natural knowledge made him a model for Renaissance comprehensive magicians like Agrippa, who sought to create unified magical systems integrating astrology, alchemy, talismans, and natural philosophy.

Albertus and the Islamic Philosophical Tradition

Albertus Magnus stands at a critical juncture in the transmission of Islamic natural philosophy to the Renaissance. He engaged deeply with Averroes (Ibn Rushd), Avicenna (Ibn Sina), and Al-Ghazali, whose writings on natural philosophy, causation, and celestial influence profoundly shaped medieval thought.

Through Albertus, later Renaissance thinkers accessed Islamic discussions of:

  • Causation and natural influence: How causes operate at a distance
  • Astrological theory: Detailed accounts of planetary effects on sublunary things
  • Hidden properties: Theoretical frameworks for understanding sympathetic action and occult qualities
  • The role of intelligences: How celestial intelligences direct natural processes

Albertus thus represents a crucial point in the long genealogy connecting Islamic natural magic to Renaissance occult philosophy. Later magicians understood themselves as recovering and developing traditions that had already been synthesized in medieval scholasticism through engagement with Islamic philosophy.

Albertus and Thomas Aquinas: Theological Stakes

Thomas Aquinas, Albertus’s most famous student, inherited and developed his teacher’s natural philosophy. Yet Thomas was more cautious about magical implications. Where Albertus explored hidden properties and sympathetic correspondences, Thomas emphasized divine omnipotence and the limitations of natural causation. Thomas was concerned to preserve God’s supernatural action against mechanical naturalism.

This tension became important in the Renaissance. When magicians cited Albertus on sympathetic magic and hidden properties, they were mobilizing medieval thought in ways that Thomas would have resisted. The Thomistic tradition emphasized the boundary between natural and supernatural; Renaissance magicians tended to expand the domain of natural magic to encompass operations that Thomists worried threatened divine action.

Understanding Albertus requires understanding this fracture point: medieval natural philosophy provided resources for both natural magic and for theological resistance to it. Later readers chose differently from these resources.

Historiographic Significance

Albertus Magnus exemplifies several critical dimensions of medieval-to-Renaissance magic transmission:

1. Authority and reception: Albertus’s actual texts were far less magical than Renaissance readers believed, yet his authority shaped how later thinkers approached magic. Historiography must attend to how figures are received and interpreted, not merely to their original intentions.

2. Natural philosophy and magic: Albertus shows that the medieval distinction between natural philosophy and magic was porous and contested. His investigations of hidden properties, sympathetic action, and celestial influence could be appropriated for magical purposes while remaining within natural philosophy frameworks.

3. Islamic knowledge and European magic: Albertus represents the crucial role of Islamic natural philosophy in providing conceptual resources for European magic. Understanding Renaissance magic requires understanding its Islamic philosophical genealogy.

4. Scholastic theology and occultism: Albertus demonstrates that magical philosophy could be pursued within scholastic theological frameworks. The question of whether hidden properties and sympathetic correspondence were compatible with Christian theology became crucial for later magicians seeking to legitimize their work.

Conclusion

Albertus Magnus himself may not have been primarily a magician; yet his natural philosophy provided crucial resources for Renaissance magic. His engagement with hidden properties, celestial influence, sympathetic correspondence, and comprehensive natural knowledge made him a foundational authority for magicians seeking to ground their operations in natural philosophy rather than demonic compulsion.

Later occultists claimed Albertus as one of their intellectual ancestors—sometimes justifiably, sometimes through misreading or misattribution. Yet the misreadings are historically important: they show how Renaissance thinkers understood Albertus as addressing the central problems of magic—how nature’s hidden operations could be understood and manipulated through knowledge of correspondences and sympathetic action.

Understanding Renaissance magic requires understanding its medieval philosophical inheritance through figures like Albertus Magnus. Renaissance magicians were not merely inventing new systems; they were working from medieval natural philosophy, appropriating and extending concepts of hidden properties, celestial influence, and sympathetic correspondence. Albertus stands as a crucial link in this genealogy, connecting medieval scholasticism to Renaissance occult philosophy.
