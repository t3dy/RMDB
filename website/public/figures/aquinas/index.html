Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274): Scholastic Philosophy and the Boundaries of Natural Knowledge

Life and Intellectual Authority

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), Dominican friar and the most influential medieval theologian, stands as a towering authority for subsequent discussions of magic, natural philosophy, and the boundaries of legitimate knowledge. Though Aquinas himself did not write treatises on magic, his philosophical framework—particularly his doctrine of causation, his theory of natural knowledge, and his insistence on divine transcendence—shaped how medieval and Renaissance thinkers debated whether magic was natural, demonic, or impossible.

Born in Sicily to a noble family, Aquinas entered the Dominican order and studied under Albertus Magnus. He became a master of theology at Paris, wrote voluminously on theology, philosophy, and biblical interpretation, and died while traveling to the Council of Lyons. His Summa Theologiae became the authoritative synthesis of Christian doctrine and Aristotelian philosophy, establishing a framework within which subsequent thinkers—including Renaissance magicians—had to operate.

Actor Terminology: Aquinas and Natural Philosophy

Aquinas presented himself as a theologian synthesizing faith and reason, revelation and Aristotelian natural philosophy. He engaged seriously with questions of causation, knowledge, and the operations of nature within a framework explicitly directed toward defending Christian doctrine.

Crucially, Aquinas was suspicious of claims about hidden powers and operations. His philosophy emphasized:

  • Univocal causation: All effects in the natural world result from natural causes operating according to principles of form and matter
  • Divine transcendence: God acts supernaturally through miracles, not through hidden natural operations
  • Limits of natural knowledge: Humans can know natural operations only through reason applied to sensible experience
  • Demonic limitation: Demons cannot truly cause effects but only manipulate existing natural causes through deception

This framework was fundamentally hostile to magic as an operative art. If all natural effects result from natural causes, then claims to command hidden powers through occult knowledge appear suspect.

Aquinas on Causation and Natural Operation

The centerpiece of Aquinas’s philosophy relevant to magic is his doctrine of causation. Aquinas, following Aristotle, distinguished between:

Formal causation: The principle that makes something what it is (its essential nature or form)
Material causation: The underlying substance or matter
Efficient causation: The agent that produces change or effect
Final causation: The purpose or goal toward which something aims

Every natural effect results from efficient causes operating according to the formal principles of the things involved. An alchemist heating mercury cannot transmute it into gold because mercury’s formal principle (its essential nature) cannot be fundamentally altered by mere material manipulation.

This doctrine directly opposed magical causation. If all effects follow from natural forms, then claims to transmute substances or to compel spirits through knowledge of correspondences appear to confuse the order of nature or to ascribe to humans powers they cannot possess.

On hidden properties: Aquinas acknowledged that some effects appear to result from hidden properties rather than obvious qualities. But he insisted these hidden properties are still natural—they follow from the essential forms of substances, accessible to knowledge through experience and reason, not through magical operation.

Aquinas on Divine Action and Miracles

Crucial to Aquinas’s anti-magical stance is his doctrine of divine action. God alone acts supernaturally, transcending the natural order. Miracles are God’s direct intervention in nature, suspending natural laws. No creature—human or demonic—can perform miracles or act outside natural causation.

Demonic causation: Aquinas argued that demons cannot truly cause natural effects. They can manipulate existing causes (moving objects, producing sensible illusions) but cannot create or fundamentally alter the natural order. Demons work through natural means, not through supernatural power.

This undermines magical claims to demonic alliance. If demons cannot grant supernatural power and can only manipulate natural causes, then invocations of demons cannot produce effects beyond what natural manipulation achieves.

Aquinas’s Influence on Medieval and Renaissance Magic Debates

Though Aquinas lived before the Renaissance, his philosophical framework became the standard against which Renaissance magic had to defend itself. Key Renaissance figures engaged directly with Aquinas:

Pico della Mirandola explicitly cited Aquinas’s philosophy to defend Kabbalah and magic. Pico argued that magic, properly understood, operates through natural means (celestial influence, sympathy, correspondence) and does not require demonic alliance. This was a defense against Aquinas’s skepticism: Pico conceded the framework but argued magic fit within it.

Agrippa similarly invoked Aquinas while modifying his conclusions. Agrippa acknowledged that natural magic must work through natural causes but argued that the hidden properties and correspondences governing nature were extensive and powerful enough to justify magical operations.

The very fact that Renaissance magicians felt compelled to engage with Aquinas shows his authority. Magic required philosophical legitimation against Aquinas’s skepticism.

Historiographic Significance: Aquinas and the Limits of Natural Knowledge

From a historiographic perspective, Aquinas exemplifies a crucial tension in medieval and Renaissance thought:

The closure problem: If all natural effects result from natural causes according to essential forms, and if hidden properties and correspondences are themselves natural, then what prevents expanded claims about what humans can know and manipulate through natural philosophy?

Aquinas’s answer was: reason, revelation, and humility. Humans can know only what they can learn through experience and reason. Claims to secret knowledge inaccessible to ordinary investigation appear suspicious and potentially demonic.

Yet this closure was unstable. Renaissance thinkers—especially those influenced by Neoplatonism and Hermeticism—expanded the domain of natural causation to include astrological influence, sympathetic correspondence, and the operations of celestial intelligences. They remained, formally, within Aquinas’s natural philosophy framework while radically extending what counted as “natural.”

Reception history: Aquinas’s influence on Renaissance magic is partly negative. His skepticism about hidden powers and claims to special knowledge provided a standard against which magic had to defend itself. Later anti-magical writers (Weyer, Scot, Peters) invoked Aquinas’s philosophy to critique magic and witchcraft.

Aquinas and Medieval Magic Theory

Aquinas engaged directly with medieval demonology and magic theory:

On demonic knowledge: Aquinas argued that demons, being incorporeal intelligences, know through intuitive understanding, not through discursive reasoning like humans. They can perceive future contingencies (though they cannot determine them—God alone is omniscient). Yet they remain limited: they cannot read human thoughts and can be deceived.

On natural magic: Aquinas acknowledged that some medieval practitioners claimed knowledge of hidden properties and natural operations. He conceded that such knowledge might exist but maintained skepticism about claims to possess it. The burden of proof lay on magicians to demonstrate that their operations were genuinely natural, not demonic.

On ritual: Aquinas was suspicious of elaborate ritual and invocation. True natural causation requires only application of natural causes; elabor rituals smack either of illusion (the magus manipulating observers’ expectations) or of demonic alliance (the ritual appealing to demons rather than commanding natural forces).

Later Influence: Aquinas and Anti-Magic

By the 16th-17th centuries, critics of magic and witchcraft invoked Aquinas to demonstrate that magical claims exceeded what nature allows. The Malleus Maleficarum (though more Scotist than Thomist) drew on Aquinas’s philosophy to argue that witches’ claims to power exceeded natural causation and therefore implied demonic alliance.

Johann Weyer, defending the possibility of natural magic while critiquing witchcraft prosecution, cited Aquinas’s philosophy to establish boundaries: what can natural knowledge and operation achieve? Weyer appealed to Aquinas’s rationalism against both credulous witchcraft believers and against magicians claiming impossible powers.

Conclusion

Thomas Aquinas stands as a foundational philosophical authority whose framework both enabled and constrained Renaissance magic. His synthesis of Aristotelian natural philosophy with Christian theology provided the conceptual vocabulary through which Renaissance thinkers had to argue.

Aquinas himself was skeptical of magic—his philosophy erected significant barriers against claims of operative magic. Yet his framework was not absolutely prohibitive. Renaissance magicians could argue, following Aquinas, that hidden properties, celestial influences, and sympathetic correspondences were natural and therefore legitimate subjects for investigation and operation.

Understanding Renaissance magic requires understanding Aquinas: not as a figure engaged in magical philosophy himself, but as a towering authority whose skepticism magic had to address and whose philosophical framework magic had to work within or against. The very fact that Renaissance magicians engaged so seriously with Aquinas shows his continuing intellectual dominance in the period.

Aquinas represents the medieval-to-Renaissance transition in thinking about knowledge, nature, and causation. His insistence on the limits of natural knowledge and his skepticism about claims to hidden power shaped subsequent debates about whether magic was natural philosophy or demonic delusion—a question that remained contested throughout the early modern period.
