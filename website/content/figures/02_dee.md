## What Dee Called His Work: The Mathematics of Nature

John Dee (1527–1609), an English mathematician, astrologer, and natural philosopher born in London, explicitly positioned his intellectual work within the framework of *mathematica*—the science of quantity, proportion, and geometric truth. His foundational text, the *Monas Hieroglyphica* (1564), bears the subtitle *Libellus Sapientiae*, and throughout his writings Dee describes himself as recovering ancient mathematical wisdom (*prisca sapientia mathematica*) encoded in the sacred geometry of creation itself. He called his work *philosophia naturalis*—natural philosophy—understood as the systematic investigation of nature through mathematical principles.

Yet this self-description masks a complexity that has troubled scholars for centuries. Dee practiced astrology, collected magical manuscripts, performed divination, experimented with alchemy, and conducted what he called "conversations with spirits" through crystal gazing and divine invocation. He wrote on talismans, the virtues of stones, the correspondence between celestial and terrestrial bodies. When hostile critics (including Queen Elizabeth's advisors) called Dee a "magus" or "sorcerer," they were identifying real practices—though Dee himself resisted the label, insisting that all his operations were grounded in the natural properties of things, the mathematical harmonies underlying creation, and lawful philosophical investigation.

This tension—between Dee's self-identification as a *mathematicus* and *philosophus naturalis* and modern historians' identification of him as a central figure in "Renaissance magic"—is the historiographic problem that frames understanding of Dee. Like Ficino, Dee would not have accepted the label. Unlike Ficino, Dee's case is more complex because his work genuinely spanned mathematics, natural philosophy, practical magic, and what would later be called early modern science.

## What Scholars Call It: From Occultism to Complexity

Modern scholarship universally positions Dee as a key figure in Renaissance magic and Elizabethan occultism. Yet scholarly understanding of what this means has evolved dramatically.

### The Mathematical-Hermetic Integration (1990s–2000s)

Noel Clulee's groundbreaking study *John Dee's Natural Philosophy* (1988) fundamentally shifted understanding of Dee. Clulee showed that Dee did not separate mathematics from magic, did not see hermetic philosophy as degrading mathematical truth, but rather understood mathematics, geometry, number, and proportion as the fundamental reality underlying both celestial mechanics and magical efficacy. For Dee, the *Monas Hieroglyphica* was not mathematics decorated with hermetic symbols; it was the expression of a unified natural philosophy in which mathematical proportion was the vehicle through which divine order manifested in creation.

Contemporary scholars (Patrick Curry, Anthony Grafton, Deborah Harkness) recognize that Dee's mathematics was not "ahead of its time" in the sense of prefiguring modern mathematics, but rather embedded in a different intellectual framework entirely. Dee's geometry was sacred geometry; his mathematics was theurgic mathematics. He was not a proto-scientist who dabbled in magic, but a natural philosopher whose natural philosophy was magical in the sense that it sought to understand and activate the hidden correspondences and proportions woven into creation by divine order.

### Contemporary Understanding (2000+)

Contemporary scholarship treats Dee as a genuinely complex figure whose work cannot be reduced to either "scientist" or "magician." The consensus recognizes:

**Dee as mathematician:** His mathematical contributions were real but specialized. He championed Euclidean geometry, wrote on navigation and surveying, contributed to Elizabethan scientific advancement. But his mathematics was embedded in a hermetic-neoplatonic framework that modern historians must take seriously, not dismiss.

**Dee as natural philosopher:** His project was to understand nature through mathematics and proportion. This included what modern scholars would call magic—talismanic practice, astrological medicine, divination—because these were legitimate expressions of natural causation within his framework.

**Dee as political actor:** Increasingly, scholars (especially Harkness) recognize that Dee's work was shaped by patronage structures, his dependence on Elizabeth's favor, his political vulnerability as a suspected Catholic sympathizer, and his need to position his work within acceptable intellectual frameworks. His mathematical emphasis was partly genuine intellectual conviction and partly strategic positioning—a way to make occult philosophy defensible to hostile authorities.

**Dee as network builder:** Recent work emphasizes Dee's role in creating and maintaining a community of scholars, practitioners, and patrons engaged in the systematic investigation of nature. This network included mathematicians, alchemists, astrologers, and philosophers. Dee's own work was inseparable from his role as connector and synthesizer of these diverse traditions.

## Major Works: Mathematics, Magic, and Philosophy

### *Monas Hieroglyphica* (1564)

Dee's most ambitious work is the *Monas Hieroglyphica* (*The Hieroglyphic Monad*), a slender volume of approximately 25 pages that presents a single symbol (*monas*) containing all the mathematical and mystical knowledge necessary to understand creation.

The monad itself is a geometrical figure combining the symbols of the Sun, the elements, and the planets into a unified design. The text explains this symbol with reference to the proportions underlying all creation: the divine unity manifesting as numerical ratio and geometric harmony. Through understanding the monad's proportions, Dee claims, a natural philosopher can understand how celestial influences operate through mathematical law, how substances possess hidden virtues accessible to the informed investigator, how the human soul corresponds to and participates in divine truth.

The *Monas Hieroglyphica* is simultaneously a mathematical treatise, a work of hermetic philosophy, an astrological manual, a mystical meditation, and a magical text. It resists reduction to any single category. Modern readers often struggle with it because they expect it to be one or the other. But Dee understood all these dimensions as expressions of a unified natural philosophy grounded in mathematical proportion.

The *Monas Hieroglyphica* was influential among continental scholars, translated into multiple languages, and treated as a significant work of natural philosophy. Yet it was also controversial, precisely because it blended mathematics with magic in ways that troubled scholars trying to separate rational science from superstitious practice.

### Mathematical and Scientific Writings

Dee wrote extensively on mathematics, particularly geometry. His preface to Euclid (1570) was influential in establishing Euclidean geometry as central to English natural philosophy. He wrote on surveying, navigation, and the mathematical principles underlying cartography. He contributed to the scientific advancement of Elizabethan England by promoting mathematical training and geometric thinking.

However, Dee's mathematics was always directed toward understanding nature—and "understanding" for Dee meant recognizing the divine proportions woven through creation, the correspondences between celestial and terrestrial bodies, the hidden virtues accessible to the prepared investigator. His mathematics was not instrumentalist (useful for practical purposes like surveying) but revelatory (showing forth divine order).

### Magical Manuscripts and Practical Operations

Dee's library (dispersed but partly preserved) contains numerous magical manuscripts: texts on talismanic practice, on the virtues of herbs and stones, on divination and crystallomancy (crystal-gazing), on ritual invocation and the summoning of spirits. Some of these were Dee's own compositions; others were copies of classical and medieval magical texts.

In the last decades of his life, Dee's work became increasingly practical. He conducted experiments in alchemy, attempting transmutation of base metals into gold. He engaged in what he called "conversations with spirits" through crystallomancy, with his associate Edward Kelley, seeking divine revelation and knowledge of hidden things. He compiled elaborate magical rituals for summoning celestial intelligences and receiving divine instruction.

Modern scholars debate how seriously to take these practices. Some argue that Dee was genuinely attempting practical magic, that he believed spirits could be invoked and commanded through proper ritual. Others suggest that Dee's crystallomancy was a mode of mystical meditation or divine illumination, not literally summoning external spirits. All agree that Dee understood these practices as continuous with his earlier mathematical-philosophical work: all were expressions of natural philosophy investigating the hidden correspondences and proportions woven through creation.

## Historiographic Debates: Mathematics and Magic, Science and Superstition

### The Science-Magic Boundary Question

A central historiographic debate concerns whether Dee should be understood as an early scientist corrupted by magic or as a natural philosopher whose natural philosophy was magical. The classical approach treated mathematics as the rational core and magic as irrational accretion. Clulee and subsequent scholars have shown this distinction to be anachronistic: Dee did not separate them. For Dee, mathematics was the language through which natural magic operated, and magic was the practice of understanding and activating mathematical proportions woven through nature.

This has implications for understanding the Scientific Revolution. If Dee's mathematical work is embedded in hermetic philosophy, then the relationship between Renaissance magic and the Scientific Revolution becomes more complex. Dee was not "on the way to" modern science but operating within a genuinely different framework. Yet his promotion of Euclidean geometry, his mathematical training, his commitment to proportion and harmony as fundamental to nature—these contributed to intellectual contexts in which modern science eventually emerged.

## Conclusion

Dee exemplifies the complexity of Renaissance natural philosophy—a figure who genuinely spanned mathematics, natural philosophy, practical magic, and what would later be called science. Understanding Dee requires recognizing that these dimensions were not opposed but integrated within a unified framework grounded in the belief that mathematical proportion was the key to understanding and manipulating nature.
